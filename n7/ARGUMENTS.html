Seven-vertex arguments

Seven vertices: first case map and transferable arguments

12 September 2026. This is a separate investigation in n7/, on branch codex/n7-unfolding. The six-vertex arguments and Lean work are not modified. The new arguments below are written proofs under self-review; no formal or independent mathematical verification is claimed.

What is covered now

We consider full-dimensional convex three-dimensional polytopes with seven genuine vertices. Cuts must be original edges. Face interiors must be disjoint in the resulting connected planar net; boundary contact is allowed. A graph case is solved only if the argument covers every convex realization of that graph, not just a convenient symmetric example.

There are 34 combinatorial types. They split as follows, with priority from top to bottom so that no graph is counted twice:

Thus 10/34 whole graph types are covered; 24 remain open. This is a combinatorial count, not a percentage of shapes, proof difficulty, or elapsed research. The 10 covered cases use published results, independently of the new six-vertex manuscript. The other 20 reductions do not depend on the six-vertex existence theorem either: their general proof is given below.

The overview draws every graph and lists a specific candidate cut tree and all remaining face-pair obligations. The census data retain vertex labels, facets, edge lists, and the exact paths used by the pair reductions. IDs such as N7-A992 use stable Graph Atlas indices; degree sequences alone are not used to identify graphs.

Why this is the complete graph list

A convex three-polytope has a simple, planar, 3-connected graph, and conversely Steinitz’s theorem realizes every such graph. Its planar embedding is unique up to reflection, so its facet cycles are determined by its graph. We filter the complete Graph Atlas through these conditions, rather than sampling coordinates. A. A universal vertex works for any number of vertices

Theorem. If a convex polytope has a vertex vv joined by an original edge to every other vertex, cutting precisely those edges gives a nonoverlapping net.

Each segment vuvu is a shortest surface path: it attains the Euclidean distance between its endpoints, which is a lower bound for every surface path. The segments have disjoint interiors and form a spanning tree. Their unfolding is therefore the shortest-path star at vv. The established star theorem allows the source to be a vertex: see Definition 1 and Theorem 4 of Kiazyk and Lubiw (2015). Nothing in this application uses n=6n=6. It settles the nine indicated types.

B. The dome theorem also has no vertex-count restriction

A dome has a base facet sharing an edge with every other facet. Retain those hinges and cut all remaining edges. Pinciu (2007), Theorem 1 and Corollary 2 prove nonoverlap for this face-neighborhood unfolding. There are two dome graphs here; the hexagonal pyramid is already in A. The additional case is N7-A875, whose base is the pentagon with labels 1, 3, 2, 4, 5 in our atlas embedding. The catalogue’s facet witness, rather than this descriptive label, is the source of truth.

C–D. Move a few whole faces; the rest keeps a known safe placement

Call vv a cofacial source if every vertex shares an original face with vv. Adjacent vertices automatically satisfy this condition. We extend the six-vertex quadrilateral comparison to an arbitrary number of vertices and to several incident polygons.

Cofacial-core theorem. At a cofacial source vv:

  1. Cut every original edge incident to vv.
  2. For each original face incident to vv, choose exactly one boundary edge not incident to vv to remain as a hinge; cut the other such boundary edges.

These cuts form an original-edge spanning tree. In the resulting net, every pair of faces outside the nontriangular faces incident to vv has disjoint interiors. Any overlap must involve at least one of those movable polygons. The theorem does not say that any hinge choice makes the movable polygons safe.

Proof

First make a comparison unfolding, temporarily allowing face diagonals. In each face containing vv, draw the diagonals from vv to all its other nonadjacent corners. Together with the original edges at vv, these are straight segments to all other vertices. They are shortest surface paths. Their interiors do not intersect, so cutting them gives a nonoverlapping shortest-path star.

A nonneighbor can share at most one face with vv. Otherwise two distinct facets would intersect in two nonadjacent vertices, which is impossible: their intersection must be a common vertex or edge. Also an original facet cannot have a graph edge as a diagonal in its interior. Thus a face with mm corners at vv contributes exactly m−3m-3 distinct nonneighbors and the same number of comparison diagonals.

The fan triangulation of each incident face consists of leaf triangles in the comparison unfolding’s uncut dual tree. Each such triangle has two cut sides at vv and one boundary hinge away from vv. Remove all the triangles belonging to the nontriangular incident faces. Deleting leaves from a tree leaves the other faces connected, with their same safe relative placements.

In the proposed original-edge net, each removed polygon is a single leaf, attached by its one retained boundary hinge. Removing these whole leaves leaves exactly the same connected core and the same hinges as before. The core’s development is unique up to a planar rigid motion, so all its pairs stay safe.

For completeness the original cuts really form a spanning tree. For an incident face v,a,x1,…,xk,bv,a,x_1,\ldots,x_k,b, the vertices a,ba,b are neighbors of vv and each xix_i is a distinct nonneighbor belonging only to this face. Deleting one edge of the chain a,x1,…,xk,ba,x_1,\ldots,x_k,b leaves two paths, each attached at one end to the radial star. Doing this in every incident face adds each nonneighbor once and creates no cycle. All vertices are connected and there are d(v)+∑k=n−1d(v)+\sum k=n-1 cuts. All are original edges. This also shows directly why leaving the comparison diagonals cut would be an invalid substitute for the required original-edge net.

Exactly what the eleven quadrilateral cases gain

For a degree-five source at seven vertices there is only one nonneighbor. If it is cofacial with the source, their common facet is a quadrilateral: a larger facet would contribute more than one nonneighbor. The two possible retained hinges are precisely the six-vertex comparison’s two routes. All pairs avoiding that quadrilateral are safe, without curvature assumptions. Additional pairs follow from the two local facts below.

For the other nine cofacial types, the same proof handles the complete set of incident polygons. In the displayed candidates, one type moves a single pentagon, six move two quadrilaterals, one moves a quadrilateral and a pentagon, and N7-A1005 moves three quadrilaterals. The candidate shown in the overview minimizes the remaining pair count within this finite family, not among all possible proof techniques or all geometric cut rules.

Two local reductions that remain valid at seven vertices

Uncut vertex fan. If all faces on the unique uncut hinge path from face AA to face BB contain the same original vertex xx, they form a consecutive fan there. Its angle is at most the full angle at xx, strictly below 2π2\pi. Convexity puts each face inside its corner wedge. These successive wedges have disjoint interiors, so AA and BB cannot overlap. Merely sharing a label is insufficient: every face and hinge along the path must retain that vertex.

Two hinges through an original face. If the path is A−Q−BA-Q-B, the relative placement is exactly a subplacement of Pinciu’s unfolding of the neighborhood of the original facet QQ. Hence AA and BB are safe. This does not authorize using an arbitrary auxiliary triangle as QQ, or a path with extra hinges.

The census checks all 25,942 original cut trees across the 34 graphs with these two tests. They alone do not settle an extra graph beyond A–B. They do give a concrete short residual list for every remaining graph. For cofacial candidates we add the core theorem before reporting that list.

E. Two core graphs and two original-edge restrictions

The four graphs without any cofacial source are:

Graph Structure Further decomposition
N7-A1212 Pentagonal bipyramid Five two-face patches around its two poles
N7-A1205 Capped-octahedron graph Octahedron graph with a new vertex over a triangular face
N7-A1167 One quadrilateral, otherwise triangles Either diagonal completes it to one of the two preceding graphs
N7-A1104 Two quadrilaterals, otherwise triangles Its four triangulations give the same two core graphs

These are graph completions, not unfoldability reductions. A diagonal inserted in an original quadrilateral is a flat auxiliary hinge and must remain uncut. Likewise, deleting the cap vertex from the capped-octahedron graph does not justify appending a tetrahedral cap to an arbitrary safe six-vertex net. The replacement faces can collide with remote faces. A cap-attachment lemma would need to prove that missing geometric step.

The separate bipyramid arguments extend several of the old octahedral lemmas. They cover three complete conditional patch patterns and additional curvature-restricted families. The bipyramid graph as a whole remains open here.

What does not automatically extend from the six-vertex proof

  • The four-patch exhaustion in Lemma F does not cover a five-patch fan. There are more separated patch pairs and longer intervening chains.
  • Lemma L’s local geometry extends with an explicit curvature threshold; the proof that the old choice automatically meets that threshold used four, rather than five, equator vertices.
  • The paired-pole rule’s wider-angle identity closes a three-petal block using the one remaining petal. A five-petal fan leaves two petals outside that block. The old identity cannot simply be cited with a renamed index.
  • Removing a degree-three vertex or merging two vertices does not by itself transfer an edge unfolding back to the original polytope.
  • The legacy seven-vertex experiment keys some samples by degree sequence. Our catalogue does not reuse that identification: different graphs can have the same degrees. The old archived bipyramid solver runs returned unknown; they supply no proof premise here.
